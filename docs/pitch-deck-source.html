Your voice, understood.

A voice-first assistant that lets Malayalam-speaking people who work with the public talk with customers in English, Hindi or Tamil, and practise the phrases they need.

github.com/nav1106/SwarasahayiOpen source · runs in a phone browser

Problem: people who serve customers all day cannot always understand them, or be understood.

  • Fish sellers, auto drivers, salon staff and college office staff speak Malayalam. Their customers often speak English, Hindi or Tamil.
  • The words that matter most are the small ones: a price, a time, a number, an instruction. A wrong number costs money or trust.
  • Existing tools expect typing, reading and English menus. Many of these workers learn and work by ear, in noisy places, on a phone, with one hand free.
  • I saw this at my own college: the non-teaching staff in the placement department are Malayalam speakers who struggle to talk with visiting recruiters.
Swarasahayi · Problem2

Solution: speak in Malayalam, be heard in their language, and learn as you work.

Talk · live translator

  • Tap the big mic and speak Malayalam. The phone says it in English, Hindi or Tamil.
  • The customer answers; the worker reads and hears Malayalam.
  • Repeat, slow replay, and "Type instead" are one tap away.

Learn · spoken tutor

  • 3 levels of useful phrases per profession. Hear it, say it, get scored word by word.
  • Next level unlocks after 80% of the last one.
  • Progress stays on the phone.

Swarasahayi · Solution3

How it stays trustworthy when a mistake would cost money.

1

Reviewed phrase bank first. 56 hand-written phrases answer instantly and work offline.

2

Clean-up. Malayalam typed in English letters and trade words (for example fish names) are normalised.

3

Translation model only for everything else.

Number guard

Every number must match in both languages, in digits or words, before the phone speaks. A mismatch is blocked and flagged.

Confidence check

Not sure what was heard? It asks again. Half sure? It asks "Is this what you meant?" before translating.

The logic is covered by 64 automated tests, including contrast checks for light and dark mode.

Launch plan: small, in person, measured.

1

Now. Working prototype on Vercel and Docker, public on GitHub.

2

Weeks 1 to 4. Native speakers review every Malayalam, Hindi and Tamil phrase. Pilot with the placement-office staff at my college.

3

Weeks 5 to 12. Two fish markets and auto stands in one district, about 20 workers, with their consent.

4

Month 4 on. Replace the hosted services with open models, publish the reviewed phrase bank, add more phrases and trades.

How we will know it works: fewer wrong-number mistakes, conversations finished without outside help, phrases passed per week in Learn, and workers still using it in week 3.

Swarasahayi · Launch plan7

What changes, and the main risk.

What changes

  • A worker can serve a customer without waiting for someone who speaks English.
  • Prices and times are checked, so fewer disputes.
  • Workers pick up the phrases they use most, at their own pace.

Main risk

  • A wrong translation of a price or instruction.
  • Handled by reviewed phrases, the number guard and ask-again checks. Real-world accuracy is not yet tested with users, and I say so.
  • Privacy: no audio is saved. Chrome sends speech to its own service to recognise it, and the consent screen says so.
